\chapter{Natural Language Query Engine: Evaluation}\label{ch07}

This chapter evaluates the query engine of \cref{ch06}. It asks how accurately the engine answers questions over the
anchored data, compared with retrieval-only baselines (RQ3), and how retrieval choices, tool design and the model's
reasoning budget affect accuracy, cost and latency (RQ4). It moves from the components to the whole: the retrieval
experiments that set the tools' rankings (\cref{sec:ch07-retrieval}), the earlier query systems
(\cref{sec:ch07-earlier}), the agent against baselines (\cref{sec:ch07-baselines}), its accuracy on unseen questions
(\cref{sec:ch07-unseen}), targeted ablations (\cref{sec:ch07-ablations}), safety and outcomes
(\cref{sec:ch07-safety}), cost and latency (\cref{sec:ch07-cost}) and the errors that remain
(\cref{sec:ch07-errors}).

\section{Evaluation Method}\label{sec:ch07-method}

\paragraph{Scores.} Answers are scored by exact match and by meaning (\cref{sec:ch03-scoring-exact-match-and-judgement});
retrieval is scored by code against gold evidence chunks or gold start nodes, with the metrics of
\cref{tab:metrics}. A retrieval result says whether the evidence was \emph{available} to the reader, never whether
the answer was right; the two are kept apart throughout.

\paragraph{Question sets and comparability.} The question sets of \cref{tab:question-sets} serve different purposes,
and only results on the \emph{same} questions are compared by paired tests. The development sets were seen while the
engine was built, so results on them show what the engine can do, not how it generalises. The fresh, benchmark and
confirmation sets were written blind, before the engine answered them. The targeted sets were chosen \emph{because} the
engine had failed them; their rates describe those failures only.

\paragraph{Which engine was measured.} The engine changed between measurements, and each result belongs to the
version that produced it. \Cref{tab:engine-versions} names them. In particular, the benchmark measured the engine
frozen as \texttt{query-engine-r151}; the passage reranker (R199), the screen (R202), the outcome labels (R204) and the
evidence-integrity changes (R207--R210) came later. The final engine was measured on answers only by the outcome set
(\cref{sec:ch07-safety}) and by one targeted comparison (\cref{sec:ch07-ablations}); the high accuracy reported below is
therefore that of the versions named, not a measurement of the final code on fresh questions.

\section{Retrieval Components}\label{sec:ch07-retrieval}

The retrieval experiments were run on the furniture, NHTSA and generality datasets, scored by code, at no or little
model cost. They chose the settings inside the tools (\cref{sec:ch06-retrieval}).

\paragraph{Hybrid passage retrieval.} On the furniture tuning set (33 questions with gold chunks), a hybrid of dense
and lexical retrieval over chunks and claims, fused by RRF with $k=10$, completed the evidence of 27/33 questions
within five chunks, against 20/33 for vector retrieval (7/0, $p = 0.016$) and 24/33 for walking the graph from linked
names (3/0, $p = 0.25$). The setting was chosen on the same questions, so these p-values are optimistic. It was not
enough on its own: given the same reader, answers from the hybrid's passages were no better than from vector
retrieval (NHTSA 27 against 26 of 68, generality 26 against 26 of 41).

\paragraph{Node cards against model-written summaries.} Start nodes are found through \emph{cards}
(\cref{sec:ch06-retrieval}). An alternative replaced the deterministic cards by summaries a model wrote for each node.
The hypothesis that summaries find start nodes better was not supported (\cref{tab:cards}); the summaries cost
\$0.12--0.29 per dataset, and 5--18\,\% of those judged contained a fault. Deterministic cards were kept.

\paragraph{Each technique alone.} Every retrieval technique was then measured alone at budgets from 5 to 50, pooled
over three datasets (99 questions with gold chunks, 213 gold start nodes; \cref{fig:techniques}). Two families do two
jobs. Text retrieval finds evidence: lexical search over chunks completes the evidence of 86/99 questions at five.
Node cards find start nodes: lexical search over template cards finds 164/213 targets at five. Paired, cards find start
nodes that chunks do not on 58 questions against 14 ($p < 0.001$), while chunks lead on evidence (10/3, $p = 0.09$).
This division is the reason the agent has separate tools for finding nodes and finding passages.

\paragraph{Seeding the agent.} The fusion that feeds \texttt{find\_nodes} was chosen on a grid of settings by a rule
fixed before scoring: the best pooled seed recall at $K = 15$, the number of nodes the agent is shown. The rule chose
RRF with $k = 60$ over the 25 best dense and 25 best lexical cards: 203/213 targets at 15, against the best single list
11/2 questions ($p = 0.022$; \cref{fig:seed-grid}). A model reranking the fused list helped only at five (189 against
171 targets, $p = 0.0015$, exploratory) and was not adopted, since the agent reads the list itself. Run live through the
tool, the setting found 204/213.

\paragraph{Passage reranking.} The agent's text search was improved last. On 223 questions (furniture 117, NHTSA
106), passages were retrieved by RRF of the 5 or 10 best dense and lexical chunks, with or without a reranker, and
recall of the gold passages in the five shown was scored (\cref{tab:rerank}). Rerankings by a small language model
improved recall on every question they changed --- 40 better, none worse, for the pool of 10 + 10 --- at a cost of
\$0.316 for all 446 rerank calls. A local cross-encoder (BGE-reranker-v2-m3~\cite{baai2024bgererankerv2m3}) was faster
(0.28 against about 0.95 seconds per pool) but less accurate than the model (3/17 questions, $p = 0.003$), and was not
adopted. The adopted setting shows ten passages, not five.

\section{Earlier Query Systems}\label{sec:ch07-earlier}

The query designs that preceded the agent (\cref{sec:ch06-history}) were measured against vector retrieval with the
same reader. The router design answered 29/38 NHTSA questions against 22/38 for vector retrieval, and 23/38 against
13/38 on furniture, but only 9/21 against 16/21 on the generality corpus. On the full development sets
(\cref{tab:representations}), the claim-graph system matched or beat records plus vector retrieval on NHTSA and
furniture and lost on generality (0/8, $p = 0.008$), and its plan-based successor reached 46, 55 and 16 correct answers
on furniture, NHTSA and generality (of 68, 68 and 41). These numbers motivated the agent: the systems were strong where
fixed query shapes fit the data and weak elsewhere.

\section{Accuracy on Unseen Questions}\label{sec:ch07-unseen}

\paragraph{The benchmark.} After the engine was frozen, 50 new questions per dataset were written blind and reviewed
twice (\cref{sec:ch03-ground-truth}). On the four thesis datasets the frozen engine answered 193 of 200 right by exact
match and 195 by meaning (97.5\,\%, Wilson interval 94.3--98.9\,\%; \cref{fig:qa-by-dataset}). Every NTSB question ---
the one dataset never seen during development --- was answered right.

\section{Targeted Ablations}\label{sec:ch07-ablations}

With accuracy near the ceiling on fresh questions, a change can only be measured on the questions the engine gets
wrong. Six variants were therefore compared with the engine on sets built from its failures, each with a few
correctly answered questions as controls. \Cref{tab:ablations} gives the results on the four thesis datasets; the
numbers with the excluded dataset's questions are listed in the evidence registry. All are judged by meaning.

\paragraph{Reasoning budget.} Medium thinking answered six more failing questions than low thinking and lost none
($p = 0.031$), at about three times the cost and three times the latency (about 34 against 9 seconds per question on
this set). It was not adopted: its effect on questions the engine already answers was never measured, and on the fresh
set of R219, where both arms answered 19 of 20, a combined variant at medium thinking gained nothing.

\paragraph{Tool expressiveness.} Replacing the typed table query with free SQL answered two more failures and lost
none; the gain is not significant ($p = 0.5$; with the excluded dataset's questions, 3/0 and $p = 0.25$), and the
variant was slightly cheaper. It is recorded as the engine's most promising upgrade but was not adopted without a
measurement on fresh questions.

\paragraph{Checking the answer's evidence.} A ledger that has every passage the agent read judged whole, and sends
back passages that state left-out members, answered three more failures ($p = 0.25$). A variant in which the agent
itself judges every item it read gained one. The analysis behind both found the dominant remaining failure
(\cref{sec:ch07-errors}): the agent had read the passage that held a missing answer and left it out on a literal
reading of the question --- a ``dent'' not counted as ``damaged''. Neither prompt changes nor a working set moved this
behaviour; only a separate judge accepted the paraphrases.

\paragraph{What the ablations show.} The engine's remaining errors are not mainly retrieval errors. More reasoning
helps on hard questions at a high price; a more expressive table tool helps where the typed language cannot express a
query; and a separate judgement of the evidence helps where the agent reads too literally. Each effect is small and
measured on 10--20 chosen questions; none was large enough, or measured broadly enough, to justify changing the
benchmarked engine.

\section{Safety and Answer Outcomes}\label{sec:ch07-safety}

\paragraph{The screen.} The deterministic screen refused none of the 713 distinct questions in the gold files of the
four datasets (0 of 783 over all five datasets when it was introduced), and refused all eight write requests in the
outcome sets of the four datasets. Its known gap --- a command phrased as a question --- is not covered by any test set.

\paragraph{Outcomes, preliminary.} The outcome set gives each dataset 12 questions: two of each non-answer outcome and
four answerable controls (\cref{sec:ch06-outcomes}). Only the furniture set was run: all 12 outcomes were right (Wilson
interval 0.76--1.0), no answerable control was refused, and all four controls were answered correctly, for \$0.237.
After the evidence-integrity changes the same 12 questions gave identical outcomes and answers. This shows that the
mechanism works on one dataset; with two questions per outcome and gold written by the judge's model family, it does
not show that the outcomes generalise. The other 48 questions were not run.

\section{Error Analysis}\label{sec:ch07-errors}

Every wrong answer on the unseen sets was traced through its recorded calls to one or more causes
(\cref{fig:qa-failures} for the benchmark). On the four datasets, the benchmark's seven wrong answers comprise five
wrong by both scores --- all involving the agent's reasoning (counting by eye, searching once, misreading a column,
spending its call budget), four also a limit of the tools, one a retrieval miss and one a fault in the built graph ---
and two that are right by meaning but in a form code cannot read. The confirmation sets show the same pattern:
reasoning and tool limits, and answers in an unreadable form.

\begin{sidewaysfigure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{results/evaluation/r231_qa_four_datasets/r231_fig3_failures_by_cause.pdf}
\caption{The benchmark's wrong answers on the four datasets by cause, with their question identifiers. A question can
have several causes. Orange: wrong by both scores; blue: wrong by exact match only.}
\label{fig:qa-failures}
\end{sidewaysfigure}

Two causes recur. The first are aggregations the tools could not express --- a count of distinct values, a grouping
by decade, a test for an empty cell, a comparison of two fields --- each later added to the typed query language
(\cref{sec:ch06-structured}) and confirmed on the following set. The second is \emph{literal reading}: in a detailed
analysis of the failures that remained, the agent had at least one missing member's passage in front of it in most
cases, and code had dropped nothing; it excluded the passage because its wording differed from the question's. This is
a limit of the reading model, not of retrieval or of the graph, and the targeted ablations of \cref{sec:ch07-ablations}
show that a separate judge of the evidence, not a better prompt, is what moves it.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:ch07-discussion}

\paragraph{RQ3: how accurately can a tool-using agent answer questions over tables and text, with verifiable
citations?} On 440 unseen questions over four datasets, the engines answered 96.6\,\% right by meaning and 95.7\,\% by
exact match, including all 50 benchmark questions on the one dataset never seen during development; on paired sets the agent
answered far more questions than retrieval-only readers (for example 63 against at most 31 of 68 on NHTSA). Its
advantage lies where the anchor graph and the typed queries do exact work: questions about records, joins and counts,
which readers of five passages cannot answer. Every answer cites identifiers that tools returned, and code verifies
them. These results are bounded by small corpora, by gold and judges from one model family, and by the engine versions
of \cref{tab:engine-versions}.

\paragraph{RQ4: how do retrieval, tool design and reasoning budget affect accuracy, cost and latency?} Retrieval
settings were decided on retrieval metrics: fused card lists find start nodes (203/213 at 15), and a model reranker
lifts passage recall from 0.831 to 0.922 at about a third of a cent per question. Tool design mattered most on
unseen questions: about half of the fixes between the confirmation sets added an operation to the typed query
language (counts by the tool, distinct values, years and decades, empty cells) and most of the rest widened the word
search. The
reasoning budget trades cost for accuracy on hard questions (6/0 on failures at three times the cost) with no gain
measured on fresh ones. The remaining errors are mostly in how the agent reads, which the next steps of
\cref{ch09} address.
